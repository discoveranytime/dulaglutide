💉 Once-weekly GLP-1 · FDA-approved

Dulaglutide: the once-a-week GLP-1 that started the weekly era

Sold as Trulicity, dulaglutide is a once-weekly injectable GLP-1 receptor agonist for type 2 diabetes — FDA-approved in 2014, with a cardiovascular indication added after the REWIND trial. This page walks through how it works, the label dosing ladder, the trial evidence, and the safety picture.

📋

Prescription medicine — this is education, not advice

Dulaglutide is an FDA-approved prescription drug with real benefits and real risks. Nothing here tells you whether to start, stop, or change any medication — those decisions belong with your clinician.

01

What dulaglutide is

The weekly injection that made GLP-1s a once-a-week routine.

Dulaglutide — sold as Trulicity by Eli Lilly — is a once-weekly injectable GLP-1 receptor agonist for adults and children 10+ with type 2 diabetes. FDA-approved

It was FDA-approved in September 2014 as an add-on to diet and exercise to improve glycemic control — one of the first GLP-1s to arrive as a once-weekly dose, a format that reshaped how these medicines fit into people's lives.

In February 2020, the FDA expanded its use: dulaglutide can also reduce the risk of major cardiovascular events in adults with type 2 diabetes who have established heart disease or multiple cardiovascular risk factors — on the strength of the REWIND trial. Clinical trial

The one-sentence version: dulaglutide is a weekly GLP-1 shot for type 2 diabetes — blood-sugar control first, with a proven cardiovascular benefit layered on top.
02

How it works

Four levers, one molecule — the GLP-1 playbook.

Dulaglutide mimics GLP-1, a natural gut hormone released after eating. By activating GLP-1 receptors, it pulls four levers at once:

🩸Insulin up — only when needed. Boosts insulin release in a glucose-dependent way, so the effect tracks blood sugar rather than firing blindly.
📉Glucagon down. Lowers the hormone that tells the liver to dump more sugar into the blood.
🐌Stomach empties slower. Food leaves the stomach more gradually, smoothing post-meal sugar spikes — and contributing to the familiar GI side effects.
🧠Appetite down. Acts on brain satiety circuits, so people tend to feel full sooner and eat less.
Why weekly works: dulaglutide is a modified form of human GLP-1 linked to part of an antibody — a structure that shields it from rapid breakdown, so a single injection keeps working for the full week. Mechanistic interpretation

03

The weekly ladder

The label's dose steps — facts, not a recommendation.

The FDA label describes a stepwise weekly ladder. People typically start low and move up only if more glycemic control is needed and the dose is tolerated — always at a clinician's direction:

0.75 mg weekly · start→ 1.5 mg weekly→ 3.0 mg weekly→ 4.5 mg weekly · max

The 3.0 mg and 4.5 mg doses were FDA-approved in September 2020 for adults needing additional blood-sugar lowering beyond 1.5 mg. Each step up trades more glucose-lowering for a higher chance of GI side effects — the classic GLP-1 bargain. FDA label

Label facts, not guidance. This ladder describes what the approved label allows — it is not advice to start, increase, or stay on any dose. Dose decisions belong with the prescribing clinician.

04

The pen

One pen, one dose, no measuring — designed for people who hate needles.

Dulaglutide comes in a single-dose, prefilled auto-injector pen — each pen holds exactly one weekly dose:

  • No mixing, no measuring, no visible needle — the needle stays hidden inside the device
  • Inject once a week, any time of day, with or without food
  • Usual sites: abdomen, thigh, or upper arm — rotating spots each week
  • Each pen is single-use: one dose, then it goes in a sharps container
  • The day of the week can be changed if needed, as long as at least three days have passed since the previous dose FDA label
Why the device matters: the pen was a deliberate answer to injection anxiety — the weekly GLP-1 only works if people actually take it, and "press a button, no needle in sight" removes the biggest barrier. FDA label

05

The evidence

AWARD for blood sugar · REWIND for the heart.

🏆 The AWARD program — glycemic control

Dulaglutide's approval rested on the AWARD trials, testing it against metformin, sitagliptin, exenatide twice-daily, liraglutide, and insulin glargine. The pattern: meaningful HbA1c reductions (often around 1% or more) and modest weight loss — consistent with the GLP-1 class. Clinical trial

🫀 REWIND — the cardiovascular trial

9,901 adults with type 2 diabetes (most without established heart disease — a primary-prevention-heavy population) received 1.5 mg weekly or placebo for a median of 5.4 years:

  • MACE (nonfatal heart attack, nonfatal stroke, or cardiovascular death): 12.0% vs 13.4% — hazard ratio 0.88 (95% CI 0.79–0.99; p=0.026), a 12% relative risk reduction
  • The clearest driver: nonfatal stroke, HR 0.76 — the only component significant on its own
  • Also seen: about 1.5 kg weight loss and 1.7 mmHg lower systolic pressure
  • GI events were the most common reason people stopped the drug

This trial earned the February 2020 FDA cardiovascular indication — the first diabetes medicine shown to cut CV events in people with and without established heart disease. Clinical trial

Keep the scale honest: a 12% relative reduction on a 13.4% background rate means about 1–2 fewer events per 100 people over 5+ years — real, and modest. Relative percentages always need their absolute context.

⏱️ How fast does anything change?

Many people notice reduced appetite and better day-to-day readings within the first few weeks. More consistent, measurable changes in A1C and modest weight loss typically become clearer after one to three months of regular use — always alongside diet and activity, and always varying by individual.

06

Common side effects

Mostly gut, mostly early, mostly fade — but not for everyone.

The most common side effects are gastrointestinal — the same family seen across GLP-1 medicines:

🤢Nausea — the headline side effect, most common after starting or increasing the dose
🤮Vomiting and diarrhea — usually mild to moderate
😣Abdominal pain and indigestion
🍽️Decreased appetite — part effect, part side effect

These tend to be worst in the first weeks and often ease as the body adjusts — which is exactly why the label starts low and climbs slowly. In REWIND, GI events were the most common reason people stopped the drug. Clinical trial

Practical note from the label: persistent vomiting or diarrhea can cause dehydration, which in rare cases has led to kidney injury — staying hydrated matters, and ongoing GI trouble deserves a call to the clinician.
📣 A note on online stories. User reports sometimes describe stronger weight loss, milder or harsher nausea, or big differences versus other GLP-1s — these accounts are anecdotal and highly individual. They don't replace clinical evidence or a clinician's advice. Anecdotal
07

Serious warnings — and who should not use it

The boxed warning first. Then the rest of the label's caution list.

✅ Who it may suit

  • Adults with type 2 diabetes who need additional blood-sugar control alongside diet and exercise
  • Those who also have cardiovascular disease or multiple heart-risk factors — the group behind the CV indication
  • People who prefer a once-weekly injection over daily treatments
  • Children 10 and older with type 2 diabetes, per the pediatric approval

Always as part of a broader plan — healthy eating, physical activity — and under a clinician's supervision.

⚫ BOXED WARNING — thyroid C-cell tumors. In rats and mice, dulaglutide caused thyroid C-cell tumors at clinically relevant exposures. Whether this happens in people is unknown — but because of it, dulaglutide is contraindicated in anyone with a personal or family history of medullary thyroid carcinoma (MTC) or Multiple Endocrine Neoplasia syndrome type 2 (MEN 2).

Other label warnings

  • Pancreatitis — including fatal cases reported; persistent severe abdominal pain needs prompt evaluation. Not studied in people with a history of it.
  • Hypersensitivity — serious allergic reactions, including anaphylaxis, have occurred.
  • Gallbladder disease — including gallstones, reported with GLP-1 medicines.
  • Low blood sugar — risk rises when combined with insulin or sulfonylureas; those doses may need lowering.
  • Diabetic retinopathy complications — rapid glucose improvement can temporarily worsen eye disease; monitoring matters.
  • Slowed stomach emptying — not recommended in severe gastroparesis; it can also affect absorption of oral medicines.

🚫 Who should not use it

  • Personal or family history of MTC or MEN 2 (contraindicated)
  • Known serious allergy to dulaglutide or its ingredients
  • People with type 1 diabetes — not indicated, and DKA risk if insulin is cut
  • Children under 10 — approved for type 2 diabetes in pediatric patients aged 10 and older (AWARD-PEDS); not established below 10
  • Individuals with a history of pancreatitis
  • Those who are pregnant or breastfeeding — needs careful medical evaluation
  • People with severe gastrointestinal disease, including severe gastroparesis
The pattern: most serious risks are rare but real — which is why a GLP-1 is a clinician-supervised therapy, not a casual one. FDA label
08

Where it sits among GLP-1s

Same family, different rhythms — administration only, no efficacy ranking.

Dulaglutide was an early weekly GLP-1; the family has since grown. How they differ in routine (not which works "better" — these medicines were never compared head-to-head for that):

📅Weekly injectables: dulaglutide, semaglutide (Ozempic/Wegovy), tirzepatide (Mounjaro/Zepbound) — one shot a week. Tirzepatide adds GIP activity on top of GLP-1.
💊Daily oral: oral semaglutide (Rybelsus) — a daily pill with a strict empty-stomach routine, same molecule as the weekly shot in a different delivery.
🕐Daily injectable: liraglutide (Victoza) — the older once-daily shot dulaglutide's weekly format was designed to replace.
Dulaglutide's niche: the simplest weekly device (hidden needle, no titration dials) plus a cardiovascular indication from a primary-prevention-heavy trial — a combination none of its siblings exactly replicate.

Understanding Dulaglutide Terminology

Twelve terms, each with its scientific meaning and a plain-English mirror. Tap each term.

Scientific MeaningDulaglutide is a once-weekly glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonist developed by Eli Lilly. It is a large fusion protein — two GLP-1 analogue chains linked to an antibody fragment — engineered for a long half-life that supports weekly subcutaneous dosing. It is FDA-approved for glycemic control in type 2 diabetes and for reducing major adverse cardiovascular events in at-risk adults, with label doses of 0.75, 1.5, 3.0, and 4.5 mg weekly.
Plain EnglishDulaglutide is the medical name for the once-a-week diabetes shot sold as Trulicity. It's a lab-built protein designed to linger in the body for a full week, mimicking the natural gut hormone GLP-1. It's approved both for lowering blood sugar in type 2 diabetes and for cutting the risk of major heart events in at-risk patients, in weekly doses from 0.75 to 4.5 mg.
Scientific MeaningTrulicity is the proprietary brand name under which Eli Lilly markets dulaglutide. It refers to the same active molecule, supplied as a single-dose prefilled auto-injector pen. Brand names identify the manufacturer's specific product and device; the generic (nonproprietary) name dulaglutide identifies the molecule itself regardless of maker.
Plain EnglishTrulicity is just the brand name on the box — the medicine inside is dulaglutide, made by Eli Lilly, in their single-dose pen. "Trulicity" = the product and device; "dulaglutide" = the actual drug molecule. Same thing, different labels.
Scientific MeaningGlucagon-like peptide-1 is an incretin hormone secreted by intestinal L-cells in response to food intake. It potentiates glucose-dependent insulin secretion, suppresses glucagon release, slows gastric emptying, and promotes satiety via central receptors. GLP-1 receptor agonists are drugs that mimic this hormone at its receptor, amplifying and extending its effects far beyond the hormone's natural minutes-long half-life.
Plain EnglishGLP-1 is a natural gut hormone your intestines release after you eat — it tells the pancreas to make insulin, tells the liver to stop releasing sugar, slows the stomach, and tells the brain you're full. The catch: natural GLP-1 lasts only minutes. GLP-1 drugs like dulaglutide are long-lasting mimics that keep those signals switched on for a week at a time.
Scientific MeaningIncretins are gut-derived hormones — principally GLP-1 and GIP — that enhance insulin secretion in response to oral glucose, an effect known as the incretin effect. They account for a large share of post-meal insulin release in healthy people, and this response is blunted in type 2 diabetes. Incretin-based therapies aim to restore or amplify that meal-triggered insulin response.
Plain EnglishIncretins are the gut's "food is coming" messengers — mainly GLP-1 and GIP — that prime the pancreas to release insulin when you eat. In type 2 diabetes this messaging is weakened. Incretin drugs work by restoring that after-meal signal, so insulin arrives when food does.
Scientific MeaningHemoglobin A1c is the fraction of hemoglobin glycated by exposure to blood glucose, reflecting average blood sugar over roughly the preceding 2–3 months. It is the standard long-term marker of glycemic control in diabetes, with most guidelines targeting below 7% for many adults. Diabetes drug trials use HbA1c reduction as a primary efficacy measure.
Plain EnglishHbA1c — "A-one-C" — is a blood test showing your average blood sugar over the past two to three months. It's the main scoreboard for diabetes control, with under 7% the usual target. When trials say a drug "lowered A1c by 1%," that's the number they're talking about.
Scientific MeaningMajor Adverse Cardiovascular Events — a composite endpoint used in cardiovascular outcome trials, conventionally comprising nonfatal myocardial infarction, nonfatal stroke, and cardiovascular death (3-point MACE). It is the standard yardstick for whether a diabetes drug also protects the heart and vessels. REWIND showed dulaglutide reduced 3-point MACE with a hazard ratio of 0.88 versus placebo.
Plain EnglishMACE is the standard "did hearts fare better?" scorecard in diabetes drug trials — it bundles three bad outcomes: nonfatal heart attack, nonfatal stroke, and death from cardiovascular causes. In REWIND, dulaglutide cut this combined risk by 12% compared with placebo.
Scientific MeaningREWIND (Researching Cardiovascular Events with a Weekly Incretin in Diabetes) was the cardiovascular outcome trial of dulaglutide 1.5 mg weekly versus placebo in 9,901 adults with type 2 diabetes, followed for a median of 5.4 years. Its primary-prevention-enriched population (most participants without established cardiovascular disease) was unusual among GLP-1 trials. The primary MACE outcome occurred in 12.0% versus 13.4% (HR 0.88, 95% CI 0.79–0.99; p=0.026), supporting dulaglutide's 2020 cardiovascular indication.
Plain EnglishREWIND was dulaglutide's big heart trial: nearly 10,000 people with type 2 diabetes, half on the weekly shot, half on placebo, watched for over five years. Unusually, most participants didn't already have heart disease — so it tested prevention, not just treatment. Heart events ran 12.0% vs 13.4%, enough to earn the drug its cardiovascular approval in 2020.
Scientific MeaningThe AWARD (Assessment of Weekly Administration of LY2189265 in Diabetes) program was the phase-3 trial series supporting dulaglutide's initial approval. Across its studies, dulaglutide was compared against placebo and active comparators including metformin, sitagliptin, exenatide twice daily, liraglutide, and insulin glargine, demonstrating HbA1c reductions and modest weight loss consistent with the GLP-1 class.
Plain EnglishAWARD was the set of trials that got dulaglutide approved in the first place. It pitted the weekly shot against a lineup of established diabetes treatments — metformin, sitagliptin, twice-daily exenatide, liraglutide, insulin glargine — and showed solid blood-sugar lowering plus modest weight loss across the board.
Scientific MeaningA boxed warning (formerly "black box warning") is the FDA's most prominent safety alert, placed at the top of a drug's prescribing information to flag serious or life-threatening risks. Dulaglutide carries one for thyroid C-cell tumors observed in rodents; whether GLP-1 drugs cause these tumors in humans is unknown. The warning drives the contraindication in patients with personal or family history of medullary thyroid carcinoma or MEN 2.
Plain EnglishA boxed warning is the FDA's loudest alarm bell — a bold-framed alert at the very top of a drug's official information. Dulaglutide's warns about thyroid tumors seen in lab rodents; nobody knows if the risk applies to people, but it's serious enough that anyone with a personal or family history of the relevant thyroid cancer (MTC) or MEN 2 syndrome must not use the drug.
Scientific MeaningMedullary thyroid carcinoma (MTC) is a rare cancer arising from the thyroid's calcitonin-producing C-cells — the same cell type in which GLP-1 drugs caused tumors in rodents. Most MTC is sporadic, but it also occurs in hereditary form as part of Multiple Endocrine Neoplasia syndrome type 2 (MEN 2). Because the human relevance of the rodent findings is unknown, dulaglutide is contraindicated in anyone with a personal or family history of MTC or MEN 2.
Plain EnglishMTC is a rare thyroid cancer that starts in the thyroid's C-cells — exactly the cells where rodents developed tumors on GLP-1 drugs. It can run in families as part of MEN 2 syndrome. Since nobody knows whether the animal finding applies to humans, the rule is simple and absolute: any personal or family history of MTC or MEN 2 means dulaglutide is off the table.
Scientific MeaningGastroparesis is delayed gastric emptying in the absence of mechanical obstruction — the stomach moves food into the intestine too slowly, causing nausea, vomiting, bloating, and unpredictable nutrient absorption. Because GLP-1 drugs deliberately slow gastric emptying as part of their mechanism, they are not recommended in patients with severe gastroparesis, where the effect could compound an already-impaired process.
Plain EnglishGastroparesis means a stomach that already empties too slowly on its own — causing nausea, vomiting, and bloating. Since GLP-1 drugs intentionally slow the stomach as part of how they work, adding one on top of severe gastroparesis would be piling slowness onto slowness. That's why the label steers clear of it.
Scientific MeaningAn auto-injector pen is a prefilled, single-use delivery device that administers a fixed dose of medication subcutaneously with a button press, concealing the needle from the user. Dulaglutide's device contains exactly one weekly dose, requires no reconstitution or dose measurement, and is discarded after use. Device design is a recognized factor in adherence for injectable therapies.
Plain EnglishAn auto-injector pen is a prefilled, one-shot device: press it against the skin, push the button, and it delivers the full dose while the needle stays hidden. Dulaglutide's pen holds exactly one weekly dose — no mixing, no measuring — and gets tossed after use. The design exists for one reason: making a weekly injection feel as un-scary as possible.
